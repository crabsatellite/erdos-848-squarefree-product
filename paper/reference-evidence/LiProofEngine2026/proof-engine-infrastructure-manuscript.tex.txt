\begin{abstract}
AI systems can rapidly produce proof sketches, programs, formalization
fragments, solver instances, and candidate proof strategies. These materials do
not have a uniform evidentiary status. This paper introduces \emph{Proof Engine
Infrastructure}, an architecture for converting untrusted generation into
independently checkable mathematical claims.

The framework has two coupled levels. At the evidentiary level, each claim is
associated with a supporting artifact, a checking procedure, the scope of that
check, and any remaining assumptions. At the inferential level, claims and
proof obligations form a typed directed hypergraph. Alternative routes have
disjunctive semantics, while obligations requiring several premises have
conjunctive semantics. Closure is therefore a derived property of the graph,
not a status asserted in prose.

The central distinction is between a positive candidate and an \emph{earned}
result. A persuasive argument, a stored certificate, or an isolated formal
theorem may support a candidate without yet licensing its use as a premise.
Every value, structure, or theorem that would contribute positive graph income
must combine reference grounding, machine-checkable evidence, and an exact
binding between the checked object and the stated claim. Promotion is admissible
only when these obligations hold together with strength preservation,
compositional completeness, and dependency preservation. The checking
technology may vary without weakening this invariant. Failed routes remain
typed negative evidence without disproving their output claims, while a
separation of generative, verificatory, compositional, and publication
functions prevents agent identity or provenance from substituting for
mathematical evidence.

Verification-cost minimization remains the design objective, extended from the
cost of checking an individual artifact to the cost of composing heterogeneous
evidence across the claim graph. The framework distinguishes earned research
closure from the stronger state of publication closure. It is motivated by a
kernel-checked Hamilton
classification and a mixed-boundary Rado-number study, but its contribution is
a general methodological framework rather than a fixed software pipeline, a
new solver, a proof assistant, or an autonomous mathematician. The framework
specifies invariant requirements for evidentiary status and derived closure;
its concrete architecture must vary with the discipline, proof object, and
available verification boundary.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

AI-assisted mathematics is often framed as a question about model capability:
can a system prove theorems, solve olympiad problems, or translate informal
proofs into a proof assistant?  These are important questions, but they do not
exhaust the methodological problem. Reliable research also requires procedures
for checking generated material, recording failed approaches, tracking changing
artifacts, and making the final evidence independently accessible.

The framework starts from the premise that provenance does not determine
evidentiary status. A generated object can support a mathematical claim only
through a stated verification procedure of known scope. Proof Engine
Infrastructure associates generated objects with such procedures, represents
their logical dependencies in a claim graph, controls status transitions, and
preserves the resulting positive and negative evidence.

For a positive result, however, checker acceptance is not sufficient by itself.
The checked object must be grounded in the intended definitions, reference data,
or formal dependencies, and an inspectable bridge must show that the machine
checked the same claim that the research argument consumes. This requirement is
stable even when the checking technology changes.

This distinction matters because the dominant evaluation instruments for
AI-for-mathematics are model-centric or benchmark-centric.  Theorem-proving
systems are scored on proof search, autoformalization accuracy, or competition
performance \cite{polu2020gptf,wu2022autoformalization,yang2023leandojo,xin2024deepseekprover,deepmind2024alphaproof}.
These measure the strength of a generator on a task distribution.  They do not
measure whether the surrounding research system can take a generator's output
and convert it, at low cost, into something a third party can independently
check.  That conversion is the object of study here.

This paper develops the architecture at the methodological level. Separate papers
report the Hamilton classification \cite{liHamiltonNCR2026} and the Rado
results \cite{liRadoNumbers2026}; the paired paper introduces a claim-level
verification framework and instantiates it on their kernel-closed and
mixed-boundary closure profiles \cite{liSatGuidedKernelArtifacts2026}; the
present paper identifies the general methodological structure shared by those
cases.

The paired paper incorporates the Rado-centered path from SAT discovery to
fast-verifiable artifacts into its broader two-case verification framework
\cite{liSatGuidedKernelArtifacts2026}. The present paper abstracts that pattern
beyond a single mathematical workload and separates the general architecture
from the claim-level framework developed in the paired paper.

The paper is also related to a separate Build Thesis manuscript
\cite{liBuildThesis2026}. That manuscript argues at the philosophy-of-science
level that mathematics's current AI acceleration should be explained through a
built coupling between AI generation and cheap mechanical verification, not
through structural conditions alone. The present paper gives that claim a
methodological form by specifying the components of a proof engine.

\subsection{Scope and non-goals}

This is a methodology paper.  It does not propose a new SAT solver, a new proof
assistant, a new certificate format, or a finished software platform.  It does
not claim that every mathematical artifact should be forced into one
verification system. Concrete mathematical examples provide evidence and
motivation; their proofs remain in the corresponding case papers. The framework
also does not prescribe one universal implementation. Its invariants concern
evidence and inference, while its engineering realization is expected to vary
with the research object.

\section{Artifacts, Not Agents, as Units of Evaluation}
\label{sec:agent}

An AI agent may propose proof strategies, write encoders,
produce proof sketches, translate definitions, search examples, or summarize
failed attempts. None of these actions establishes a mathematical claim. The origin of an
artifact does not determine its mathematical status.

\begin{claim}[Agent provenance is insufficient]
No output becomes mathematical evidence merely because it was generated by an
AI agent. It becomes evidence only after it has passed an independent
verification procedure with stated scope.
\end{claim}

The resulting design objective is to support multiple fallible generation
processes while making their mathematical outputs inexpensive to verify
independently.

This distinguishes a proof engine from a benchmark solver. A benchmark
solver may demonstrate model capability on a task distribution.  A proof
engine must record the development of mathematical claims through generation,
checking, failed approaches, remaining assumptions, formal verification,
publication, and later revision. A benchmark score evaluates a model on a task
set; a proof engine produces a body of independently checkable artifacts.

A more capable generator may propose better strategies, write cleaner encoders,
and produce more nearly correct formal fragments. Each output nevertheless
requires an appropriate check before it can support a mathematical claim. The
cost of that check is a property of the surrounding infrastructure as well as
of the generator. Model improvement primarily lowers the cost of
\emph{generation}; the architecture is designed to lower the cost of
\emph{verification}.

\begin{definition}[Functional role]
A functional role is a contribution to the formation or assessment of a
mathematical claim. Roles describe what is being done---generation,
verification, composition, or publication---rather than who or what performs
it. A role is therefore not a trust rank.
\end{definition}

Table~\ref{tab:roles} gives a minimal separation of functions. A human or
machine participant may perform more than one function, but performing one
function does not confer the epistemic authority of another.

\section{The Generation-Verification Interface}
\label{sec:interface}

\begin{definition}[Generation-verification interface]
A generation-verification interface is the part of a research infrastructure
that exposes the evidentiary record of each substantive claim to the larger
claim graph.
\end{definition}

The companion paper defines the corresponding local object as a
\emph{verification profile}: a claim, its representing object, the checking
procedure, the scope of the check, the remaining assumptions, and a stable
identifier \cite{liSatGuidedKernelArtifacts2026}. The present paper does not
repeat that claim-level taxonomy or its case-specific checks. It asks the next
architectural question: how should many such local records be composed into a
research state whose closure can be derived?

The interface is therefore a logical boundary between local evidence and
global inference. It accepts heterogeneous verification profiles without
equating them, exposes the exact proposition that each profile licenses, and
prevents a local status from silently becoming a stronger graph-level claim.

\section{Positive Evidence: Reference Grounding and Machine Checking}
\label{sec:positive}

A verification profile says how an artifact was checked. A proof engine needs a
stronger invariant for any value, structure, or theorem that would contribute
positive evidence to the larger graph: the checked artifact must also be
grounded in the intended research object and bound to the exact claim for which
status is sought.

\begin{definition}[Reference grounding]
Reference grounding for a claim \(C\) is a versioned specification of the object
to which \(C\) refers. It records the governing definitions and normalization,
the provenance of any input or reference data, the relevant dependency set,
and the baseline cases or calibration facts used to establish that the
representation is the intended one.
\end{definition}

Reference grounding is domain dependent. For a finite mathematical value it may
contain the instance family, input data, known cases, and encoding convention.
For a formal theorem it may contain the exact definitions, imported theory, and
the correspondence between the paper statement and the formal statement. For
an empirical object it may contain raw observations, calibration data, and the
measurement protocol. These objects are not interchangeable, but each fixes
what is being checked.

\begin{definition}[Machine-checkable evidence]
Machine-checkable evidence for \(C\) is a versioned artifact, an independently
executable checking procedure, and an acceptance record produced under a
declared trust and dependency policy.
\end{definition}

The machine-checkable artifact may be a proof term, certificate, witness,
exhaustive computation, symbolic derivation, or independently rerunnable
analysis. The framework does not require one proof assistant, solver, or
certificate language. It requires that the selected mechanism expose an
acceptance relation whose scope is adequate for \(C\).

\begin{definition}[Claim binding]
A claim binding is an inspectable bridge showing that the reference-grounded
object and the machine-checked object express the exact claim \(C\), with the
same domain, definitions, normalization, endpoints, and declared assumptions.
\end{definition}

Let \(G(C)\), \(M(C)\), and \(B(C)\) denote reference grounding, machine
acceptance, and exact claim binding. The positive-evidence obligation is
\[
  \operatorname{PositiveEvidence}(C)
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  G(C)\land M(C)\land B(C).
\]
Reference data without a checker documents an object but does not establish the
new result. A machine proof without reference grounding may certify the wrong
encoding or an unintended formal statement. A semantic bridge without an
accepted machine artifact establishes correspondence but not correctness. The
three obligations are therefore conjunctive.

\begin{principle}[Technology-independent positive evidence]
Every value, structure, or theorem that changes claim-graph reachability must
be supported by reference grounding, machine-checkable evidence, and an exact
claim binding. The technology realizing the machine check may be replaced
without changing the principle, provided that the replacement verifies the
same claim at no weaker scope and exposes its trust assumptions.
\end{principle}

\begin{definition}[Equivalent checking realizations]
Two machine-checking realizations are equivalent for a claim \(C\)
when, relative to the same reference grounding, each produces independently
replayable evidence for the same proposition, scope, and dependency policy. A
replacement is admissible only if the new realization satisfies these same
conditions or establishes a stronger result.
\end{definition}

This permits engineering evolution without evidentiary drift. A proof-assistant
kernel may replace a certificate checker, a certified checker may replace raw
solver replay, or an independently implemented verifier may replace an earlier
script. The graph need not privilege the original technology; it must preserve
the proposition established, the reference object, and every remaining
assumption.

\section{Claim Graphs and Derived Closure}
\label{sec:claimgraph}

The generation--verification interface describes the evidentiary status of
individual artifacts. A theory of research closure must additionally represent
how claims depend on one another, which arguments are alternatives, and which
partial results must be combined.

\begin{definition}[Claim graph]
A claim graph is a directed acyclic hypergraph
\[
  \mathcal G=(\mathcal C,\mathcal E),
\]
where each vertex \(C\in\mathcal C\) is a typed mathematical claim and each
hyperedge \(e\in\mathcal E\) has a finite input set \(I_e\subseteq\mathcal C\),
one output claim \(o_e\in\mathcal C\), and a declared evidentiary status.
\end{definition}

Several edges with the same output encode alternative proofs and therefore
have OR semantics. A single edge with several inputs encodes an assembly
obligation and therefore has AND semantics. Let \(R_0\) be the declared root
claims and let \(\mathcal E_{\mathrm{earned}}\) be the earned edges. Define
\[
\begin{aligned}
R_{k+1}=R_k\cup
\{\,o_e:\ e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{earned}},\ I_e\subseteq R_k\,\},
\qquad
\operatorname{Reach}(\mathcal G)=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}R_k .
\end{aligned}
\]
A target claim \(T\) is research-closed relative to the declared root claims
exactly when
\(T\in\operatorname{Reach}(\mathcal G)\). Minimal open route sets, dependency-
ready obligations, and unavailable premises can then be derived from the graph
rather than inferred from a narrative description.

\begin{principle}[Derived closure]
A claim is closed only when it is reachable from the declared roots through
earned edges. Neither confidence, authorship, nor the local success of an
uncomposed argument changes this relation.
\end{principle}

\subsection{Edge lifecycle and negative evidence}

The lifecycle separates the existence of positive evidence from its
inferential use. A proposed edge begins open. Mathematical scrutiny or an
appropriate checker may establish different forms of candidate evidence. The
admissibility rule in Section~\ref{sec:earned} determines whether that evidence
is sufficient for the edge to become earned. A refuted or insufficient
argument becomes retired.

Retiring one argument does not disprove its output claim when other incoming

it positive evidentiary force.

\section{Candidate-to-Earned Admissibility}
\label{sec:earned}

The distinction between candidate evidence and an earned edge is the central
inferential boundary of the framework.

\begin{definition}[Admissible promotion]
The promotion of an edge \(e\) from candidate to earned is admissible when the
output has reference grounding, an accepted machine-checkable artifact, and an
exact claim binding; when every claim used in composition preserves its meaning
and strength; when the components cover the full obligation; and when every
assumption on which the conclusion depends remains visible.
\end{definition}

Let \(G(e)\), \(M(e)\), \(B(e)\), \(S(e)\), \(C(e)\), and \(D(e)\) denote,
respectively, reference grounding, machine acceptance, claim binding, strength
preservation, compositional completeness, and dependency preservation. The
abstract promotion rule is
\[
 e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{earned}}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 G(e)\land M(e)\land B(e)\land S(e)\land C(e)\land D(e).
\]
The right-hand side is conjunctive: satisfying only a proper subset leaves the
edge in a candidate state. Table~\ref{tab:gates} states the interpretation of
these predicates.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.25\linewidth}
>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.63\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Condition & Theoretical requirement \\
\midrule
Reference grounding \(G(e)\) & The value or structure is identified by its
definitions, normalization, reference inputs, provenance, and applicable
baseline cases. \\
\addlinespace
Machine acceptance \(M(e)\) & A versioned artifact passes an independently
executable checker under a declared trust and dependency policy. \\
\addlinespace
Claim binding \(B(e)\) & The grounded object and the checked object express the
exact output claim at the required scope. \\
\addlinespace
Strength preservation \(S(e)\) & The statement established by each component
implies the statement consumed by its parent under the same definitions and
normalization. \\
\addlinespace
Compositional completeness \(C(e)\) & The components cover the whole
obligation, and no case, endpoint, overlap, or quantitative contribution is

\addlinespace

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The candidate-to-earned rule is an all-or-nothing condition on
inferential use, not a ranking of how promising a candidate appears.}
\label{tab:gates}
\end{table}

The conditions address distinct logical failures. Reference material alone
does not prove a new positive value. A mechanically accepted artifact may encode
the wrong object or have a narrower scope than the claim that cites it. A
correct local lemma may leave cases uncovered. Quantitative arguments may fail
when the same contribution is used twice. A conditional result cannot become
unconditional by omitting its condition. These are instances of a general
failure of promotion, not separate sources of partial closure.

\begin{principle}[All-or-nothing promotion]
An edge becomes earned only when every condition required for its inferential
use holds simultaneously. Otherwise it remains a candidate, regardless of how
many conditions have already been established.
\end{principle}

\subsection{Research closure and publication closure}

An earned edge may establish a conditional research result while retaining an
explicit external assumption. This is useful: the graph can represent the
advance without describing it as an unconditional theorem. Publication closure
is stronger because it additionally requires a public statement whose wording,
evidence, assumptions, and verification boundary agree. Separating these states
prevents a locally correct component from being advertised as closure of a
stronger public claim.

\section{Invariant Framework, Adaptive Architecture}
\label{sec:adaptation}

Proof Engine Infrastructure is not a claim that every research problem should
use the same tools, decomposition, or workflow. It is a claim that different
realizations should preserve the same methodological invariants: claims must be
typed; positive results must combine reference grounding, machine-checkable
evidence, and exact claim binding; composition must preserve strength and
assumptions; and closure must be derived rather than asserted.

The architecture is therefore parameterized by its \emph{epistemic object}.
Across disciplines, the relevant object may be a mathematical theorem, an
experimental measurement, a statistical estimate, or a verified program.
These objects require different notions of evidence, error, replication, and
closure. Even within mathematics, a finite classification, a symbolic identity,
an asymptotic estimate, and a proof-assistant theorem do not generate the same
verification obligations.

This distinction also clarifies the role of engineering examples. A finite
combinatorial classification may be organized around exhaustive case
generation, versioned reference instances, compact witnesses, and a
kernel-checked characterization. A mixed analytic--computational problem may
instead require reference datasets, encoders, certificates, independently
checked witnesses, analytic lemmas, and an explicit inventory of residual
assumptions. A highly branching proof search may benefit from a finer claim
graph, systematic negative evidence, recursive strength checks, and quantitative
non-duplication checks. These are examples of adaptation, not mandatory
components of every proof engine.

\section{Verification-Cost Minimization}
\label{sec:vcm}

\begin{principle}[Verification-cost minimization]
Among architectures that preserve the required evidentiary status, prefer the
one that lowers the total cost of independent verification and composition.
\end{principle}

The motivating asymmetry is that AI may lower the cost of generating candidate
outputs faster than the cost of verifying them
\cite{liVerificationAsymmetry2026}. The present paper translates that
cross-domain observation into a design objective for mathematical research:
minimizing the cost of independently checking and composing claim-bound
evidence. At the level of one claim, the companion paper's
economy-of-verification principle selects the least burdensome adequate check
\cite{liSatGuidedKernelArtifacts2026}. At the architectural level, local choices
interact: an individually cheap check may create an expensive semantic bridge,
an incompatible representation, or a dependency that must be reverified by
every parent claim.

For a claim \(C\) and an adequate boundary \(b\), let \(V_b(C)\) denote the cost
to an independent reader of checking the corresponding evidence. Let \(K(e)\)
denote the additional cost of establishing that the inputs of edge \(e\) are
jointly sufficient for its output. For a declared target \(T\), an architecture
seeks a reachable earned subgraph \(\mathcal G_T\) with low total cost
\[
  \sum_{C\in\mathcal C(\mathcal G_T)} V_{b(C)}(C)
  + \sum_{e\in\mathcal E(\mathcal G_T)} K(e),
\]
subject to the admissibility conditions in Section~\ref{sec:earned}. This is a
design objective, not a claim that verification effort is already measured on
a universal numerical scale.

The second term matters. A collection of locally checked lemmas can remain
difficult to assess if their definitions differ, their scopes do not align, or
their assumptions are not propagated. Conversely, a moderately expensive proof
object may reduce total cost if it removes many repeated checks. Verification
cost is therefore a property of the composed architecture, not merely of an
individual checker.

\section{Verification-Boundary Allocation}
\label{sec:boundaries}

The paired case paper defines and compares concrete verification boundaries,
including witness, certificate, kernel, dependency, and archival checks
\cite{liSatGuidedKernelArtifacts2026}. Here the architectural problem is not to
repeat that taxonomy but to allocate heterogeneous boundaries across a claim
graph.

A boundary allocation is a partial map \(b:\mathcal C\to\mathcal B\), where
\(\mathcal B\) is the set of checking procedures available in the relevant
domain. It is valid only when \(b(C)\) is adequate for \(C\) and the output of
that boundary can enter every proof edge that consumes \(C\) without changing
the claim's meaning or dropping assumptions. Different claims in the same
argument may therefore use different boundaries.

No boundary is privileged independently of the proof object. Kernel checking
may be appropriate for a formal theorem, certificate checking for a finite
refutation, direct validation for a witness, and independent replication for a
computational or empirical object. The architecture must make these differences
composable. The same pluralism applies across disciplines: changing the
epistemic object changes the admissible boundary family and may change the
shape of the claim graph itself.

\section{A Two-Dimensional Reference Architecture}
\label{sec:layers}

The framework has two dimensions that should not be collapsed into one linear
pipeline. The \emph{inferential dimension} is the claim graph: it records which
premises jointly support which conclusions. The \emph{evidentiary dimension}
records how the artifact attached to each claim has been checked. A third
operation, publication, projects an earned part of this structure onto a stable
public surface. Figure~\ref{fig:layers} summarizes the relation.

\section{System-Level Consequences}
\label{sec:patterns}

The claim-graph view adds consequences that do not appear at the level of an
individual verification profile.

\begin{principle}[Local positivity is not global closure]
A checked local result changes the status of the target only when it enters an
earned edge on a complete route from the declared roots.
\end{principle}

This prevents a formalized lemma, an exhaustive computation on a proper
subdomain, or a persuasive conditional argument from being mistaken for the
stronger claim that consumes it.

\begin{principle}[Negative evidence is edge-relative]
A refuted or insufficient argument changes the status of that argument, not
automatically the truth status of its proposed conclusion.
\end{principle}

The failed edge can remain valuable when its assumptions, method, and
obstruction are reproducible. Other incoming edges may still establish the
same output claim. This locates negative knowledge without turning a failed
method into a false theorem.

\begin{principle}[Closure-respecting publication]
The evidentiary status stated in a publication must be the image of an earned
subgraph under a projection that preserves claims, assumptions, and
verification boundaries.
\end{principle}

Publication may omit exploratory history, alternative routes, and internal
coordination records, but it must not strengthen the surviving claims or erase
their dependencies. The companion paper develops the corresponding
claim-level publication and reproduction procedure; the present principle
states the graph-level constraint on that procedure
\cite{liSatGuidedKernelArtifacts2026}.

\section{Relation to Model-Centric and Benchmark-Centric Work}
\label{sec:related}

Model-centric systems ask how capable a model is.  Benchmark-centric systems
ask how well a model performs on a task set.  Proof Engine Infrastructure asks
a different question: what architecture turns generated mathematical work into
artifacts whose evidentiary status and checking cost are explicit?

\paragraph{AI theorem proving and proof search.}
A large body of work studies neural and LLM-based proof generation and search,
from early generative theorem proving \cite{polu2020gptf} to
retrieval-augmented and large-scale systems
\cite{yang2023leandojo,xin2024deepseekprover} and competition-level systems
\cite{deepmind2024alphaproof}.  These systems target the generation layer.  The
present paper is complementary: it studies the surrounding architecture that
assigns the generator's outputs to verification boundaries.

\paragraph{Autoformalization.}
Autoformalization translates informal statements and proofs into a proof
assistant \cite{wu2022autoformalization,szegedy2020autoformalization}.  This is a
means of producing input for formal verification, but a translation remains a
generated artifact. It supports a formal claim only after its output has been
kernel-checked and its assumptions audited.

\paragraph{Proof assistants and libraries.}
Proof assistants and their libraries supply the kernel-checking boundary
\cite{demoura2021lean4,mathlib2020}.  Our claim is not novelty at this
component level; it is that kernel checking is one boundary among several, and
that the methodological contribution is an architecture specifying which
claims are assigned to which boundary.

\paragraph{SAT, certificates, and verified checking.}
SAT-based mathematics and certified proof checking
\cite{heule2016pythagorean,heule2018schur,wetzler2014drat,cruzfilipe2017lrat,lammich2017grat,gocht2022veripb}
supply the finite-verification and certificate-checking boundaries.  Again the
contribution is not a new solver or format; it is the placement of these
boundaries inside one generation-verification interface alongside formal and
archival boundaries.

Stronger models can improve candidate proofs, and stronger checkers can lower
verification cost. The present claim is that generation capability alone does
not characterize a research infrastructure. Such an infrastructure must also
verify, record, classify, select, and publish generated outputs together with
their verification records.

\section{Relation to the Build Thesis}

The existing Build Thesis manuscript \cite{liBuildThesis2026} and the present
paper address different levels of analysis. The Build Thesis advances a general
explanatory claim:
mathematics appears unusually accelerated by AI because an infrastructure build
has coupled generation to cheap mechanical verification.  It is a
philosophy-of-science and infrastructure-studies argument about why structural
conditions are not enough.

Proof Engine Infrastructure gives this thesis a methodological specification.
It identifies reference grounding, machine-checkable positive evidence, exact
claim binding, claim graphs, candidate-to-earned admissibility, functional
separation, verification-boundary allocation, reusable negative evidence, and
closure-respecting publication as elements of the build. It also explains why
the invariant method admits different engineering architectures for different
research objects.

\section{Relation to the Case Studies}
\label{sec:companion}

The publication sequence has three distinct levels. The mathematical papers
establish the Hamilton and Rado results \cite{liHamiltonNCR2026,liRadoNumbers2026}.
The paired paper defines verification and closure profiles, then reports the
claim-level evidence, statuses, public artifacts, and reproduction procedures
for those two cases \cite{liSatGuidedKernelArtifacts2026}. The present paper
does not repeat their theorem statements, audit outputs, artifact inventories,
or commands. It uses the contrast only to test an architectural proposition:
the invariants remain stable while the implementation changes with the proof
object.

In the Hamilton case, a uniform mathematical construction and a
kernel-checkable final classification permit a comparatively concentrated
verification architecture: reference-grounded formal definitions and theorem
maps are paired with kernel checking, while exploratory finite computation need
not remain a premise. In the Rado case, versioned finite objects, analytic
arguments, search results, constructive witnesses, and formal dependency
records require heterogeneous machine boundaries and a more explicit
representation of remaining assumptions. These are engineering realizations of
the framework, while the detailed evidence for each realization belongs to the
paired case paper.

\section{Limitations}

This paper is methodological. It does not claim a new SAT solver, proof
assistant, certificate format, or universal proof-engine implementation. The
framework has informed engineering realizations using machine-readable claim
graphs, automated status audits, and checked promotion rules, but these
realizations are examples rather than parts of the definition.

The cost objective is qualitative; the paper does not report a universal cost
scale or a controlled comparison against a model-centric method. The Hamilton
and Rado cases demonstrate contrasting realizations, not cross-field
generality. Independent implementations, replications, and measurements are
needed to evaluate productivity and portability.

Finally, the framework does not mechanically establish novelty, importance, or
the semantic equivalence of every informal sentence and formal statement.
Machine checking establishes only the acceptance relation of the declared
checker; the reference grounding and claim binding still require mathematical
or domain review. Human review therefore remains necessary.

\section{Conclusion}

A proof engine is an infrastructure for converting generated mathematical
materials into claims whose evidentiary and inferential status can be checked
independently. Local verification profiles describe what each artifact
establishes. A typed claim graph describes how those local results compose, and
closure is derived only through edges whose positive outputs have reference
grounding, machine-checkable evidence, and exact claim binding, and whose
composition preserves strength, completeness, and dependencies.

The framework fixes these invariants but not one engineering stack. Artifact
types, reference packages, graph granularity, checkers, quantitative controls,
and publication surfaces should change with the discipline and proof object.
The technology is replaceable; the obligation to ground, check, and bind every
positive result is not. The Hamilton and Rado realizations illustrate this
adaptability; their mathematical and reproducibility details remain in the
paired case paper. Together with graph-level verification-cost minimization and
the separation of research from publication closure, the framework supports
auditable AI-assisted research without treating generation capability or agent
identity as mathematical trust.
